# SHAP interpretation — why the confusable classes confuse

Generated by `python -m src.explain`, which writes `shap_full.json` /
`shap_full.png` / `shap_testbed.json` / `shap_testbed.png` alongside this
file. Numbers below are read from those artifacts and from the confusion
matrices recomputed from `runs/model_{track}_proba.npy` /
`_ytest.npy` (same test set `src/evaluate.py` scores).

## `full` track — SHAP corroborates the leakage finding, it doesn't add a new one

Confusion is almost nonexistent: of 12,000 test rows, only 6 are
misclassified at all (2 Policy Abuser → Fraudulent Return, 3 Policy Abuser →
Wardrobing, 1 Fraudulent Return → Policy Abuser). SHAP explains why in a way
that lines up exactly with `docs/LEAKAGE_FINDING.md`'s greedy-forward-selection
result, not by coincidence:

| class | top SHAP feature | 2nd |
|---|---|---|
| Fraudulent Return | `days_to_return` (2.22) | `wishlist_to_cart_time_hrs` (1.01) |
| Legitimate | `returns_per_order` (4.92) | `return_rate_pct` (1.74) |
| Policy Abuser | `return_rate_pct` (2.66) | `is_high_value_item` (1.31) |
| Wardrobing | `days_to_return` (2.38) | `wishlist_to_cart_time_hrs` (0.94) |

These are the same three features — `days_to_return`,
`wishlist_to_cart_time_hrs`, `return_rate_pct` (and its algebraic twin
`returns_per_order`) — that `docs/LEAKAGE_FINDING.md` identified as
reconstructing the label almost perfectly (cumulative macro-F1 0.995 from
those three alone), because the generator drew them from near-disjoint
per-class uniform ranges. SHAP is not finding a subtler signal underneath
that; it is pointing at the same three box-separating features and
confirming that essentially all of each class's predicted probability comes
from 1–2 of them. **The model is not weighing many weak behavioral signals —
it is reading the generator's decision boundary off two or three columns.**
That is the honest reading of a SHAP plot with a 5:1 ratio between the top
feature and everything else, and it is why this repo does not report `full`
track SHAP as evidence the model "understands" return abuse.

See `shap_full.png`.

## `testbed` track — where the real, and more interesting, confusion lives

With `wishlist_to_cart_time_hrs`, `days_to_return`, `return_rate_pct`,
`total_returns_lifetime` (and their proxies) removed, confusion is real and
concentrated exactly where `docs/LEAKAGE_FINDING.md`'s Day 4 finding said it
would be:

| actual → predicted | count | share of actual class |
|---|---|---|
| Legitimate → Policy Abuser | 274 | 3.3% of 8,345 Legitimate |
| Legitimate → Wardrobing | 211 | 2.5% |
| Policy Abuser → Legitimate | 129 | 9.1% of 1,414 Policy Abuser |
| Policy Abuser → Wardrobing | 68 | 4.8% |
| Wardrobing → Policy Abuser | 64 | 5.7% of 1,129 Wardrobing |
| Wardrobing → Legitimate | 52 | 4.6% |
| *(Fraudulent Return, any)* | 36 total | 3.2% of 1,112 — still the cleanest class |

Legitimate ↔ Policy Abuser is the single largest confusion pair, and SHAP
shows the mechanism: on `testbed`, the two classes' top drivers *overlap*
instead of pointing at disjoint ranges.

| class | top 3 SHAP features |
|---|---|
| Legitimate | `refund_to_avg_order_ratio`, `avg_order_value_usd`, `multiple_accounts_flag` |
| Policy Abuser | `is_high_value_item`, `refund_to_avg_order_ratio`, `total_orders_lifetime` |
| Wardrobing | `item_returned_opened`, `total_orders_lifetime`, `refund_to_avg_order_ratio` |

`refund_to_avg_order_ratio` is a top-3 driver for all three of the confusable
classes, and `total_orders_lifetime` appears in two of three. Unlike the
`full` track's near-disjoint uniform ranges, these are continuous signals the
whole customer population shares to some degree — a customer with a
middling refund-to-order ratio and a dozen lifetime orders could plausibly be
a heavy-but-honest shopper, a bracketing policy abuser, or a wardrober, and
there is no single threshold on any one of these features that cleanly
separates the three. That is a genuinely harder, more realistic problem than
the `full` track poses, which is the entire reason `testbed` exists (Rung G,
`docs/LEAKAGE_FINDING.md` "Decision (Day 2)").

Fraudulent Return stays the cleanest class even on `testbed` (top driver
`refund_to_avg_order_ratio` at 2.30, more than 1.5× its nearest competitor,
`tracking_number_valid`) — consistent with the Day 4 finding that fraud is
the easiest class to separate and that the decision layer's live axis is
approve ↔ soft-friction, not the hard-block boundary.

See `shap_testbed.png`.

## Caveat

Both explanations are read off a synthetic, generator-produced dataset (see
`docs/LEAKAGE_FINDING.md` and the synthetic-data limitation in
`README.md`/`docs/ARCHITECTURE.md` §2). SHAP here explains *this model on
this data*, faithfully — it is not a claim about which signals would matter
against real merchant returns.
